## 🔄 ROUND 1 — First Impressions

**🇺🇸 Jake:** Alright, let's get into it. For me, **PrivaType** is the instant winner. It's two familiar English words smashed together — "privacy" and "type" — and any American gets it in under a second. It sounds like "private" with a twist. It's punchy, it's modern, it feels like a product you'd see on Product Hunt with 1,000 upvotes. "Parlotype" sounds like something from a museum exhibit. "SayLocal"... it sounds like a Yelp competitor for finding restaurants near you.

**🇲🇽 Sofía:** I have a different reaction. In Spanish, "Parlotype" actually resonates beautifully. "Parlar" is an old word for speaking — we use "parlar" informally in some regions, and "parlotear" means to chatter. So "Parlotype" immediately feels like "the thing that types what you speak." It's elegant. "PrivaType," on the other hand... in Spanish, "priva" can mean "to deprive" — "privar" means to deprive someone of something, or it can colloquially mean "to get drunk" in Spain. So "PrivaType" could accidentally sound like "deprived typing" or worse. That's a concern for the Latin American market.

**🇫🇷 Antoine:** *Magnifique*, Sofía, because I was about to make a very similar point. "Parlotype" is essentially French. "Parler" means "to speak" — every French person will immediately understand this name without any explanation. It has the elegance of a French word with the clarity of a technical term. It sounds like it belongs next to words like "prototype" or "archetype." It has *gravitas*. "PrivaType" sounds too American, too marketing-driven. And "SayLocal" is... forgettable. Generic. It could be anything.

**🇩🇪 Katrin:** I will be direct. From a German perspective, "PrivaType" is the most immediately functional name. Germans value clarity and precision. "Privat" in German means "private" — it's nearly identical. And "Type" is universally understood. So "PrivaType" communicates exactly what the product does: private typing. "Parlotype" — I would not know what this means without explanation. "Parlieren" exists in German but it's archaic and slightly pretentious. Most Germans would just see a foreign word and move on. "SayLocal" is two English words, and while Germans understand English well, it doesn't evoke any particular emotion.

**🇷🇺 Dmitri:** In Russian, none of these names will translate directly — our users encounter app names in Latin script and learn them as brand names. So what matters is: how does it *sound* when a Russian says it? "PrivaType" — "При-ва-тайп" — the "priva" part sounds uncomfortably close to "привет" (privet, meaning "hello") but distorted. It's awkward on the tongue. "Parlotype" — "Пар-ло-тайп" — this flows much better phonetically. It has a rhythm. Russian speakers are very comfortable with French-origin words; our language is full of them — "парламент," "парле." "SayLocal" — "Сэй-локал" — it sounds clunky, like two unrelated words stitched together. My vote so far: **Parlotype**.

## 🔄 ROUND 2 — Deeper Arguments

**🇺🇸 Jake:** Okay, I hear the international concerns about PrivaType, but let me push back. The *primary market* for a desktop voice-to-text app is English-speaking professionals — Americans, Canadians, Brits, Australians. That's where the money is, that's where the early adopters live. In English, "PrivaType" is a *perfect* name. It tells you the value proposition in one word: your typing stays private. "Parlotype" tells you nothing to an English speaker. They'll think it's a typo for "prototype." Or a board game. The marketing burden for Parlotype in the US market is significantly higher.

**🇲🇽 Sofía:** Jake has a point about the primary market, but I'd argue that a *good name transcends borders*. If this app succeeds, it will go global. Latin America is a massive tech market — Mexico, Colombia, Argentina, Brazil (where Portuguese "parlar" also works). "Parlotype" works across all Romance languages: Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese. That's over a billion speakers. "PrivaType" works in English and German. That's it. And the "priva" problem I mentioned isn't minor — if you Google "priva" in Spanish contexts, the associations aren't great.

**🇫🇷 Antoine:** I want to add something about *brand emotion*. "PrivaType" makes me feel like I'm being sold something. It's defensive — it's saying "be afraid, protect yourself." The emotion is *anxiety*. "Parlotype" makes me feel *empowered*. It's saying "speak, and your words become text." The emotion is *capability*. In the European market especially, where GDPR has been law for years, people don't need to be reminded about privacy in every product name — they expect it as a baseline. What they want to feel is: this tool makes me more powerful. Parlotype does that.

**🇩🇪 Katrin:** Antoine, I respect the emotional argument, but I disagree. German enterprise buyers — and this tool is partly aimed at professionals — want to see *Datenschutz* (data protection) signaled clearly. A name that whispers "privacy" is a feature, not a bug. However, I will concede one point: "Parlotype" does have one advantage in German — it follows the same pattern as "Prototyp," "Stereotyp," "Archetypus." These are familiar word structures. So while the meaning isn't immediately clear, the *shape* of the word feels legitimate and professional. It wouldn't look out of place in a German software catalog.

**🇷🇺 Dmitri:** I want to make a technical community argument. In the Russian-speaking developer community — which is massive in open-source and local-first software — names that sound *unique* earn respect. "PrivaType" sounds like a hundred other privacy tools: PrivaZer, PrivacyBadger, PrivateInternetAccess. It blends into the noise. "Parlotype" sounds like nothing else. When a Russian developer sees it on GitHub, they stop and think, "What is this?" That curiosity is worth more than instant comprehension. And once they learn it, they remember it forever. It's like "Kubernetes" — nobody knew what it meant at first, and now it's one of the most recognized names in tech.

---

## 🔄 ROUND 3 — Rebuttals & Concessions

**🇺🇸 Jake:** Alright, Dmitri's Kubernetes argument actually landed. I'll concede that uniqueness matters. And Sofía's point about "priva" in Spanish is a real problem I can't dismiss. But let me make one last stand for PrivaType: *discoverability*. If someone types "private typing app" or "privacy voice to text" into Google, PrivaType has natural keyword alignment. Parlotype has zero SEO gravity — nobody is searching for "parlo" anything. You'd have to build brand awareness from scratch.

**🇲🇽 Sofía:** Jake, that's a fair SEO point, but it cuts both ways. "PrivaType" will compete for search ranking against every "private" and "typing" keyword on the internet. "Parlotype" will *own* its search results from day one because the word doesn't exist yet. Search "Parlotype" right now — you'll get nothing. That's an *advantage*. It means the app will be the #1 result immediately. With PrivaType, you're fighting for space against PrivacyTools, TypeScript, PrivateVPN...

**🇫🇷 Antoine:** I want to concede something to Jake and Katrin. For the *very first impression* in the English-speaking market, PrivaType is clearer. I acknowledge this. But names are not just about the first second — they're about the *lifetime relationship* with the brand. "Parlotype" rewards you over time. The more you use it, the more the name makes sense, the more attached you become. It becomes part of your vocabulary. "I'll Parlotype that" could become a verb — like "Google it" or "Photoshop it." Can you say "I'll PrivaType that"? It's clunky.

**🇩🇪 Katrin:** Antoine makes an excellent point about verbification. In German, we create compound verbs constantly. "Parlotypieren" — "to parlotype" — actually works grammatically in German. "Privatypen" does not. And I want to raise one more practical concern about PrivaType: in German, "Privat" carries a connotation of *personal/individual* versus *professional*. A "Privat-Telefon" is your personal phone, not your work phone. So "PrivaType" might accidentally signal "this is for personal use, not enterprise." That's a positioning problem.

**🇷🇺 Dmitri:** I'll add my final technical argument. The name "Parlotype" has excellent properties for a software product: it's one word, lowercase-friendly (parlotype), works as a CLI command (`parlotype --model whisper`), makes a clean package name (`npm install parlotype`), and the domain/GitHub namespace is likely clear. "PrivaType" has a capitalization ambiguity problem — is it "Privatype"? "PrivaType"? "Priva-Type"? In Russian developer forums, people would spell it five different ways. Parlotype has only one possible spelling.

## 🔄 ROUND 4 — Final Statements & Vote

**🇺🇸 Jake:** I came in as a PrivaType champion, and I'll be honest — you all moved me. The Spanish problem is real, the SEO ownership argument is compelling, and the verbification point is clever. My concern remains that Parlotype needs *explanation* in the English market. But I'll acknowledge: the best brand names often do. Nobody knew what "Spotify" meant either. I'm changing my vote to **Parlotype**, with the condition that the tagline does the heavy lifting: *"Parlotype — Speak. Type. Private."*

**🇲🇽 Sofía:** My vote has been **Parlotype** since the beginning and nothing has changed. It works in Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, and French. It's elegant, it's unique, and it has no negative connotations in any language I know. When this app launches in Latin America — and it should — the name will need zero localization. That's rare and valuable.

**🇫🇷 Antoine:** **Parlotype**, without hesitation. It is a name with *soul*. It respects the European linguistic heritage while being modern and technical. It positions the product as something crafted, not something assembled from marketing keywords. And selfishly — it's a little bit French, and the world could always use more of that.

**🇩🇪 Katrin:** I deliberated seriously between the two, and I choose **Parlotype**. My reasoning is structural: it is unambiguous in spelling, functions well as a technical identifier, follows established word-formation patterns in Germanic and Romance languages, and avoids negative connotations in all five of our represented languages. PrivaType is a good name for the American market specifically, but Parlotype is a good name for the *world*.

**🇷🇺 Dmitri:** **Parlotype**. Unanimous, it seems. In the open-source world, the name that makes people curious wins. "Parlotype" makes me want to click the link. "PrivaType" makes me think I already know what it does — and I'm probably wrong. Curiosity is the most valuable emotion in software marketing.
